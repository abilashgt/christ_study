# EFS vs Non-EFS on Eternal Generation

| Category | EFS (Eternal Functional Subordination) | Non-EFS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic claim | The Son is eternally equal in being with the Father, yet eternally submits in role or function. | The Son is eternally equal in being with the Father, and subordination belongs to the incarnation, not to eternal intra-Trinitarian life. |
| Eternal generation | Often affirmed, but sometimes treated as less central than eternal authority and submission. | Usually treated as the key personal distinction: the Son is eternally begotten of the Father. |
| Authority | The Father has eternal authority over the Son. | Authority language is usually tied to Christ's mediatorial mission in history, not to eternal relations of authority. |
| Obedience | The Son's obedience is seen as eternal, not only incarnational. | The Son's obedience is primarily connected to becoming man and carrying out redemption. |
| Distinction between persons | Distinction is often explained partly through authority and submission. | Distinction is explained through relations of origin: Father unbegotten, Son begotten, Spirit proceeding. |
| Main concern | Preserve ordered relations within the Trinity while affirming equality of essence. | Preserve full equality and avoid suggesting an eternal hierarchy in the immanent Trinity. |
| Historical proponents | As a named position, EFS is mainly modern rather than classical. Supporters sometimes appeal to older language of taxis or order, but EFS itself is usually not identified as the standard pro-Nicene position. | Historically, this is closer to the mainstream Nicene and pro-Nicene tradition: Athanasius, the Cappadocians, Augustine, and later Reformed orthodox theologians who emphasized eternal generation without eternal subordination of authority. |
| Current proponents | Common contemporary proponents include Wayne Grudem and Bruce Ware. | Contemporary defenders include Kevin Giles, Matthew Barrett, and theologians who stress classical trinitarianism. |
| Common criticism | Risks sounding like eternal hierarchy within the Godhead, even if essence is said to be equal. | Can be criticized for not giving enough weight to biblical language of the Son's obedience and mission. |
| Relation to eternal generation debate | Eternal generation may be affirmed, but EFS discussions often shift focus to role distinctions. | Eternal generation is central for explaining how the Son is distinct without being inferior. |

## Short Summary

EFS says the Son is eternally equal in nature but eternally subordinate in role. Non-EFS views usually say the Son's submission belongs to the incarnation and that eternal generation, not eternal submission, is the proper way to describe the Son's relation to the Father.
