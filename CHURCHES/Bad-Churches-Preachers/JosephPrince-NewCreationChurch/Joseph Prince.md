# Joseph Prince — New Creation Church

## Overview

Joseph Prince (born 1963) is the senior pastor of New Creation Church in Singapore and an internationally distributed television, publishing, and conference preacher. His message is built around the “gospel of grace,” the believer’s identity as righteous in Christ, and the finished work of Jesus. His ministry is charismatic and strongly emphasizes healing, provision, protection, and practical “victorious living.”

This note summarizes Prince’s stated theology and then records the main reasons many conservative and Reformed reviewers classify his teaching as **hyper-grace** with **Word-of-Faith/prosperity-gospel tendencies**. It is a theological assessment of published teaching, not a judgment about his motives or about every statement he has made.

## What Prince and New Creation Church officially affirm

According to the ministry’s current statement of faith, Prince affirms:

- The authority and divine inspiration of Scripture.
- One God in three persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
- The deity, virgin birth, sinless life, miracles, atoning death, bodily resurrection, ascension, and future return of Jesus Christ.
- Salvation through faith in Jesus Christ, water baptism as an outward identification with Christ’s death and resurrection, and the importance of evangelism.
- Baptism in the Holy Spirit, praying in the Spirit, and the nine gifts of the Spirit.
- Progressive sanctification: believers are fully justified and righteous in Christ, while holiness in daily life develops progressively.
- The Lord’s Supper as a remembrance of Christ’s death, practiced corporately every Sunday.

These are important orthodox affirmations. The major disagreements with Prince concern how he interprets the law, sanctification, healing, prosperity, confession, and Communion—not whether he uses orthodox Christian vocabulary.

Source: [Joseph Prince Ministries — “We Believe”](https://www.josephprince.org/about/we-believe)

## Central theological emphases

### 1. Radical “new covenant grace”

Prince teaches that Christians are under the new covenant of grace rather than the Mosaic covenant of law. He repeatedly contrasts “law” and “grace”:

- Law demands; grace supplies.
- Law exposes sin and condemns; grace gives righteousness and no condemnation.
- Law focuses on what people must do; grace focuses on what Christ has done.
- Believers are accepted because of Jesus’ obedience, not their performance.

He does not say that the Ten Commandments are evil or that Christians should deliberately sin. His official statement says the commandments are holy and good, and that true grace produces holiness, generosity, and moral transformation. Nevertheless, his presentation gives very little practical, covenantal authority to the moral law as a continuing rule of life. In Prince’s framework, the believer is to be led by a relationship with Jesus and by grace rather than by the law written on tablets.

### 2. Justification and identity in Christ

Prince strongly emphasizes the “gift of righteousness.” The believer is completely forgiven, accepted, and righteous before God because of Christ’s finished work. He teaches that believers should be more conscious of Christ’s righteousness than of their sin, guilt, or condemnation. This is intended to produce freedom from fear and addiction and to motivate obedience through love rather than legalistic effort.

He distinguishes justification from sanctification: justification is complete and cannot increase, while practical holiness is progressive. He also teaches that grace breaks sin’s dominion, so “grace” is not meant to be permission to continue in sin.

### 3. Repentance and confession of sin

Prince defines repentance primarily as *metanoia*, a change of mind—especially a change from trusting in self-righteousness to trusting in Christ’s finished work. When a believer falls into sin, he directs the person to run to Jesus, receive forgiveness, and believe that the sin was fully judged at the cross.

This emphasis can be pastorally reassuring, especially for people trapped in shame. The concern raised by critics is that Prince’s emphasis on the believer’s already-complete forgiveness can minimize the ongoing biblical place of confession, grieving sin, church discipline, and the Spirit’s convicting work (for comparison, see 1 John 1:8–10; James 5:16; 2 Corinthians 7:9–11; John 16:8).

### 4. Healing, health, and provision

Prince teaches that Christ’s atoning work includes physical healing and freedom from the curse of sickness and poverty. His ministry says that God desires believers to walk in health and wholeness and that Jesus has already paid for healing. It also teaches that God desires abundant provision and “good success,” understood holistically rather than as money alone.

His official position includes qualifications: believers should see doctors, continue medication, and not ignore professional medical advice. The ministry also denies teaching that every believer will become wealthy and rejects greed and materialism.

Even with those qualifications, the practical effect is close to prosperity/Word-of-Faith teaching because healing, financial breakthrough, protection, and success are repeatedly presented as benefits secured by the cross and available to be claimed by faith.

Sources: [Joseph Prince Ministries — healing FAQ](https://gospel-of-grace-faq.josephprince.org/article/272-how-do-i-walk-in-faith-and-wisdom-as-i-look-to-the-lord-for-healing) and [“The Gospel Brings Health and Provision”](https://www.josephprince.org/blog/daily-grace-inspirations/he-gospel-brings-health-and-provision)

### 5. Positive confession and spiritual authority

Prince teaches that believers should speak God’s promises over sickness, debt, family, and circumstances. His materials say that the believer’s words can “activate” or release God’s provision, healing, protection, and prosperity, and they encourage commanding sickness to leave and declaring debt cancelled.

This is a clear Word-of-Faith feature. It goes beyond the biblical practice of praying God’s promises when it treats spoken declarations as a mechanism that changes circumstances. Scripture does teach faith-filled prayer and truthful speech, but it also records faithful believers lamenting, suffering, asking God to remove affliction, and submitting to God’s will rather than guaranteeing a particular outcome (2 Corinthians 12:7–10; Philippians 4:11–13; James 4:13–16; 1 John 5:14).

Source: [Joseph Prince — “Speak and Step Into God’s Supply”](https://www.josephprince.org/blog/articles/speak-and-step-into-gods-supply)

### 6. Holy Communion and healing

New Creation Church teaches weekly Communion and says that, by faith, believers receive afresh the benefits of Christ’s finished work, including health, wholeness, peace, and forgiveness. The church specifically links the broken body of Christ with bodily healing and encourages a “revelation” of healing when partaking.

The positive side is a serious and frequent remembrance of Christ’s death. The concern is that Communion can be treated as a means for obtaining physical health, rather than primarily as the church’s covenant meal of remembrance, proclamation, fellowship, and self-examination (1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 11:23–29). Critics have therefore described Prince’s Communion teaching as sacramental instrumentalism or as a form of healing promise attached to the elements.

Source: [Joseph Prince Ministries — “We Believe,” section on Holy Communion](https://www.josephprince.org/about/we-believe)

## Main theological concerns

### A. Law and sanctification

Prince’s law/grace contrast rightly rejects justification by works, but it can become an overcorrection. The New Testament teaches that believers are not justified by works of the law, yet it also calls Christians to obey Christ, fulfill the moral substance of the law through love, and use Scripture for correction and training in righteousness (Romans 3:28; Romans 13:8–10; Matthew 28:20; 2 Timothy 3:16–17). The question is not whether Christians earn acceptance by keeping the law; they do not. The question is whether God’s moral will remains authoritative for the Christian life. Prince’s formulation tends to obscure that distinction.

### B. “No condemnation” and the ordinary means of repentance

The comfort of justification should not be confused with the denial of ongoing confession and repentance. A Christian can be secure in Christ and still be called to confess sin, put sin to death, receive correction, and submit to church discipline. Prince’s “sin-consciousness” emphasis risks making biblical self-examination sound like unbelief or a return to law.

### C. Guaranteed healing and suffering

The New Testament teaches that God heals and that Christians should pray for healing. It does not promise that every believer will be healed in this present age. Christians are also told to expect suffering, weakness, persecution, bodily decline, and patient endurance (Romans 8:17–25; 2 Corinthians 4:7–18; 11:23–30; 1 Timothy 5:23; 2 Timothy 4:20; James 5:13–18). Presenting sickness as a defeated curse that should inevitably disappear can burden sick people with the suspicion that they lack faith or have failed to confess correctly.

### D. Prosperity and the Christian’s cross

Prince rejects the label “prosperity gospel,” but his teaching still places substantial emphasis on health, provision, success, protection, and positive outcomes. The New Testament does promise God’s fatherly care and sufficient provision, while warning against love of money and calling believers to contentment, sacrificial generosity, and cross-bearing (Matthew 6:25–34; 1 Timothy 6:6–10; Mark 8:34–35; Philippians 4:10–13). This makes his message materially different from historic Christian teaching that treats suffering and poverty as possible aspects of faithful discipleship rather than evidence that believers have failed to access their blessings.

### E. Interpretive method

Critics also note that Prince often reads Old Testament narratives and symbols as direct pictures of Christ’s finished work and the believer’s blessings. Typology can be biblical, but it must be controlled by the text’s context and by the New Testament’s own use of the Old Testament. When symbolic connections become the main proof for healing, wealth, or effortless success, the method can turn into allegory and selective proof-texting.

## Overall assessment

Joseph Prince is not best described as a teacher who denies every central Christian doctrine. His published statement affirms the Trinity, Christ’s deity and resurrection, salvation by faith, the authority of Scripture, the Holy Spirit, and the second coming. His strongest and most attractive emphasis is the sufficiency of Christ’s finished work and the rejection of legalistic self-justification.

The decisive problem is that this grace emphasis is expanded into a comprehensive formula for victorious living: freedom from sin, physical healing, financial provision, protection, success, and changed circumstances through faith and spoken confession. His distinction between justification and sanctification is stated, but his teaching on the moral law, confession, suffering, healing, prosperity, and Communion can substantially distort the New Testament’s balance.

**Practical classification:** orthodox language and several orthodox core affirmations, combined with a significant hyper-grace emphasis and strong Word-of-Faith/prosperity features. His teaching should therefore be tested carefully against the whole counsel of Scripture rather than accepted because it frequently uses the words “Jesus,” “finished work,” and “grace.”
