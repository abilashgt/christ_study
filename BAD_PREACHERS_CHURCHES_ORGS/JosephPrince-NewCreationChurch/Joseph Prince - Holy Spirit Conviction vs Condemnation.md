# Joseph Prince — A Reformed Biblical Warning on “The Holy Spirit Never Convicts You of Your Sins”

## Executive summary

Joseph Prince repeatedly teaches that the Holy Spirit does not convict believers of their sins, but convicts them of their righteousness in Christ. This teaching should be rejected as an unbiblical distortion of grace. It confuses justification with sanctification, assurance with immunity from correction, and conviction with condemnation.

The pastoral concern behind the teaching—freedom from shame and condemnation—is legitimate. The conclusion is not. Scripture teaches that believers are not condemned in Christ, while also teaching that they are reproved, disciplined, called to confess sin, led to repentance, and sanctified by the Holy Spirit.

**Assessment:** This is best classified as **hyper-grace with antinomian tendencies**. It is a serious doctrinal error because it undermines repentance, sanctification, self-examination, church discipline, and the ordinary means by which the Spirit preserves believers in holiness. Its use of orthodox words such as “Jesus,” “grace,” “finished work,” and “righteousness” does not make the teaching sound.

## Reformed biblical warning

The church must not accept a preacher merely because he speaks warmly about grace or frequently mentions the finished work of Christ. The question is whether his teaching agrees with the whole counsel of God.

Prince’s claim creates a false opposition between grace and conviction. In Scripture, grace does not remove repentance; grace produces repentance. Grace does not make sin irrelevant; grace trains believers to renounce ungodliness and live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives (Titus 2:11–14). Grace does not silence the conscience; it cleanses the conscience so that the believer may serve the living God (Hebrews 9:14).

The dangerous result of Prince’s teaching is false assurance. If a believer is told that the Holy Spirit never points out sin, then biblical warnings may be dismissed as legalism, confession may be treated as an insult to Christ’s finished work, and persistent sin may be renamed “condemnation.” That is not Christian liberty. It is a pastoral system that can leave unrepentant people comfortable on the road to destruction.

The New Testament repeatedly commands believers to examine themselves, confess sin, put sin to death, pursue holiness, restore the wandering, and submit to correction (1 Corinthians 11:28–32; 2 Corinthians 13:5; 1 John 1:8–10; Romans 8:13; Hebrews 12:14; Galatians 6:1). A preacher who systematically removes these commands from the believer’s life is not protecting the gospel; he is weakening the gospel’s sanctifying power.

**Pastoral warning:** Christians should not sit submissively under teaching that denies the Spirit’s convicting and sanctifying work. Test the preacher by Scripture, examine the fruit of the teaching, and compare every promise of “grace” with the biblical commands to repent, obey, persevere, and endure to the end.

Joseph Prince, *Destined to Reign*, chapter 11, pp. 134–135.

## Scope and source method

This note distinguishes between:

- **Primary sources:** Joseph Prince’s books and official ministry material; Justin Peters’s own ministry statement, testimony, and recorded teaching.
- **Biblical sources:** Scripture passages evaluated in context.
- **Historical sources:** Reformation-era materials describing the antinomian controversy.
- **Secondary sources:** Critical articles or quotations that require verification against the original book or recording.

The conclusion is a theological assessment of a specific teaching, not a final judgment on every doctrine, sermon, or individual associated with Joseph Prince or New Creation Church.

Prince states that “the Holy Spirit never convicts you of your sins.” **Source location: *Destined to Reign*, chapter 11, pp. 134–135; the central sentence appears on p. 134.** His concern is that Christians may mistake condemnation and accusation for the Spirit’s work and consequently live under shame instead of resting in Christ’s finished work.

## What Prince means

Prince’s argument is that believers are already righteous and accepted in Christ. Therefore, the Holy Spirit’s ministry is not to continually condemn them as guilty or rejected, but to reveal Christ’s righteousness, grace, and finished work.

His practical distinction is:

- **The accuser says:** “You are sinful, rejected, and hopeless.”
- **The Holy Spirit reveals:** “This is wrong; come back to Christ and walk in His grace.”

Prince especially connects “conviction” with the condemning sense of guilt that drives a person away from God.

## The central biblical error: confusing conviction with condemnation

This is the fundamental error in Prince’s teaching. He takes a true statement—believers are not condemned in Christ—and turns it into a false conclusion: believers therefore cannot be convicted, reproved, or corrected concerning sin.

The Bible does not make that move.

| Prince’s error | Biblical correction |
|---|---|
| “No condemnation” means the Spirit never confronts a believer’s sin. | **Romans 8:1** removes judicial condemnation, not fatherly correction. |
| Conviction of sin must be accusation from Satan. | **2 Corinthians 7:9–10** calls sorrow over sin “godly grief” when it produces repentance. |
| The Spirit only reminds believers of righteousness. | **John 16:8**, **Romans 8:13**, and **Galatians 5:16–25** describe the Spirit’s work in relation to sin, holiness, and mortification. |
| Believers do not need to identify or confess sin. | **1 John 1:8–10** commands believers to acknowledge and confess their sins. |
| Correction threatens assurance. | **Hebrews 12:5–11** says God’s loving discipline confirms believers as sons and produces holiness. |

### Key biblical verses that expose the error

**1. The Holy Spirit convicts concerning sin — John 16:8–11**

Prince argues that “the world” means unbelievers and that the singular “sin” means only unbelief. That may describe the immediate evangelistic context, but it does not authorize the universal statement that the Spirit never exposes sin in believers. The rest of the New Testament describes the Spirit’s sanctifying work within the church.

**2. Godly sorrow is produced for repentance — 2 Corinthians 7:9–10**

Paul does not dismiss the Corinthians’ grief as condemnation. He says they were grieved into repentance and calls their sorrow “godly.” This is a direct biblical contradiction of the idea that sorrow over a believer’s sin must be from the accuser.

**3. God reproves His believing children — Hebrews 12:5–11**

The writer addresses believers as sons and says that God reproves and disciplines those He loves. The purpose is holiness, and the result is the peaceful fruit of righteousness. This is corrective conviction, not condemnation.

**4. Christ reproves a church — Revelation 3:19**

Jesus says to a church, “Those whom I love, I reprove and discipline,” and then commands repentance. Christ’s love does not exclude reproof; His love is the reason for it.

**5. Believers must confess sin — 1 John 1:8–10**

John includes himself and the church in the confession: “If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves.” The Christian response to sin is not denial, positive confession of personal righteousness, or dismissal of the Spirit’s correction. It is honest confession and reliance on God’s faithful forgiveness and cleansing.

**6. Believers must examine themselves — 1 Corinthians 11:28–32**

The Lord disciplines His people so that they will not be condemned with the world. This passage explicitly distinguishes discipline from condemnation. Prince’s framework collapses the very distinction Scripture makes.

**7. Sin grieves the Holy Spirit — Ephesians 4:30**

Paul warns believers not to grieve the Holy Spirit. The Spirit is not indifferent to the believer’s conduct. The Spirit’s presence produces holiness and exposes conduct that is inconsistent with the new life in Christ.

**8. Believers put sin to death by the Spirit — Romans 8:13**

The same chapter that declares “no condemnation” also commands believers to put the deeds of the body to death by the Spirit. Romans 8 therefore disproves Prince’s attempt to use verse 1 against the Spirit’s sanctifying work.

### The decisive distinction

> **Condemnation** is God’s judicial sentence against the guilty. For those in Christ, that sentence has been removed.
>
> **Conviction** is the Spirit’s truthful exposure of sin, bringing the believer to repentance, holiness, and restoration.

To deny condemnation is the gospel. To deny conviction is to undermine sanctification. Prince’s error is not that he teaches grace; it is that he defines grace in a way that suppresses the biblical ministry of correction.

## John 16:8 in context

Jesus says:

> “When he comes, he will convict the world concerning sin and righteousness and judgment” (John 16:8).

The following verse identifies sin as unbelief in Jesus. Prince therefore interprets this passage as describing the Spirit’s conviction of the unbelieving **world**, especially its need to believe in Christ, rather than a continual conviction of believers for every individual sin.

[John 16:8–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+16%3A8-11&version=ESV)

This interpretation has some force because Jesus specifically says “the world,” and explains sin as not believing in Him. However, the passage does not by itself prove that the Holy Spirit never corrects believers.

## Passages showing correction of believers

The New Testament also describes God correcting and restoring His people:

- God disciplines His children for their good so that they may share His holiness (Hebrews 12:5–11).
- Jesus says, “Those whom I love, I reprove and discipline,” and then calls His people to repent (Revelation 3:19).
- Paul describes “godly grief” that leads believers to repentance, while distinguishing it from destructive worldly sorrow (2 Corinthians 7:8–10).

[Hebrews 12:5–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews+12%3A5-11&version=ESV)  
[Revelation 3:19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation+3%3A19&version=ESV)  
[2 Corinthians 7:8–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Corinthians+7%3A8-10&version=ESV)

These passages show that correction, reproof, and sorrow over sin are not necessarily condemnation. They can be instruments of God’s fatherly restoration.

## Conviction versus condemnation

| Holy Spirit’s conviction | Condemnation or accusation |
|---|---|
| Specific and truthful | Vague and exaggerated |
| Identifies the wrong action | Attacks the person’s identity |
| Leads to repentance | Leads to hiding and despair |
| Restores fellowship with God | Makes a person feel rejected by God |
| Points toward Christ and grace | Makes a person run away from Christ |

Romans 8:1 says there is “no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” This means believers are not under God’s judicial rejection. It does not mean that believers never receive correction or are never called to repent.

[Romans 8:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans+8%3A1&version=ESV)

## Biblical verdict

Prince is right that Christians must not live under shame-based religion or believe that every failure cancels their acceptance in Christ. But this true pastoral concern does not excuse his false theological conclusion.

The statement “the Holy Spirit never convicts believers of sin” should be rejected. It is not merely imprecise language; it is a serious doctrinal error when used to deny Spirit-led exposure of sin, confession, repentance, and sanctification.

A more balanced formulation would be:

> The Holy Spirit does not condemn believers as guilty and rejected, but He does expose sin, correct us, and lead us into repentance and holiness.

The Spirit’s conviction is restorative, not destructive. It does not remove assurance; it proves that God is treating His people as children and brings them back into obedient fellowship with Him.

## Assessment of Prince’s teaching

The pastoral warning against condemnation is valuable, but Prince’s teaching must be rejected because it confuses condemnation with conviction. His absolute wording contradicts the broader biblical witness and weakens the doctrines of repentance and sanctification.

**Summary:** Joseph Prince should not be recommended as a sound preacher in this area. His teaching presents grace against conviction, whereas Scripture presents grace as the power by which God forgives, disciplines, sanctifies, and preserves His people.

## Comparison with the full counsel of God

Paul said that he did not shrink from declaring “the whole counsel of God” (Acts 20:27). A doctrine should therefore be tested against the full witness of Scripture, not only against one interpretation of John 16:8–11.

### Prince’s central claim

Prince’s repeated claim can be summarized as follows:

1. The Holy Spirit convicts the **world** of the one sin of unbelief.
2. The Holy Spirit convicts believers of **righteousness**, not of their sins.
3. The Holy Spirit does not come to point out a believer’s faults.
4. Condemning feelings about sin are from the accuser or the conscience, not from the Holy Spirit.

The first point is a possible interpretation of John 16:8–9. The problem is that the wider New Testament repeatedly describes God’s correction, reproof, discipline, repentance, confession, and holiness among believers.

### Strongest contradictory or qualifying passages

| Passage | What the text says | Why it challenges Prince’s absolute statement |
|---|---|---|
| **2 Corinthians 7:9–10** | Paul rejoices that the Corinthians were “grieved into repenting” and calls this “godly grief.” | These are professing Christians. Their sorrow over sin led to repentance, and Paul presents it as spiritually beneficial—not automatically satanic condemnation. |
| **Hebrews 12:5–11** | God reproves and disciplines His sons for their good, so they may share His holiness. | The recipients are believers, explicitly called sons. God’s fatherly reproof is directed toward real sin and produces righteousness. |
| **Revelation 3:19** | Jesus says to a church, “Those whom I love, I reprove and discipline,” then commands repentance. | This is direct correction of believers. It shows that loving reproof and the call to repent are compatible with being loved and accepted by Christ. |
| **1 John 1:8–10** | John writes to the church: “If we say we have no sin,” we deceive ourselves; if we confess our sins, God forgives and cleanses us. | The normal Christian life includes recognizing and confessing sin. This conflicts with any practical implication that believers should deny or ignore their sins. |
| **1 Corinthians 11:28–32** | Believers are told to examine themselves; the Lord disciplines them so they will not be condemned with the world. | Christian self-examination and divine discipline are not treated as unbelief. The text distinguishes discipline from final condemnation. |
| **Ephesians 4:30** | Believers are warned not to grieve the Holy Spirit. | The Spirit is morally involved in the believer’s conduct. Sin can grieve Him, which sits uneasily with the claim that He never points out a believer’s faults. |
| **Galatians 5:16–25** | Believers are commanded to walk by the Spirit, reject the works of the flesh, and produce the fruit of the Spirit. | The Spirit does not merely reassure believers of their status; He exposes the conflict with the flesh and leads them into holy conduct. |
| **Romans 8:13** | “By the Spirit” believers put to death the deeds of the body. | The Spirit actively confronts sinful practices in believers and empowers their mortification. |
| **2 Timothy 3:16–17** | Scripture is profitable for teaching, reproof, correction, and training in righteousness. | Since the Spirit inspired Scripture and uses it to sanctify believers, His ministry cannot be reduced to reassurance without correction. |

The most direct counter-texts are **2 Corinthians 7:9–10, Hebrews 12:5–11, Revelation 3:19, 1 John 1:8–10, and 1 Corinthians 11:28–32**. They explicitly address believers and connect spiritual health with sorrow over sin, confession, correction, discipline, and repentance.

### John 16:8–11: the limitation of Prince’s argument

John 16:8 says that the Spirit will convict “the world” concerning sin. Verse 9 explains the sin as unbelief: “because they do not believe in me.” Prince is correct that the immediate context focuses on the world’s rejection of Christ and that “sin” is singular in the verse.

However, this does not establish that the Spirit never exposes sin in believers. It establishes one aspect of the Spirit’s ministry toward the world. Other passages must be allowed to describe His ministry toward the church: reproof, discipline, sanctification, repentance, and the production of holiness.

Prince’s interpretation therefore creates a false either/or if it implies:

- either the Holy Spirit convicts unbelievers and comforts believers, or
- the Holy Spirit convicts believers and therefore condemns them.

The Bible presents a third category: the Holy Spirit can lovingly expose sin in believers without condemning them.

### Passages supporting Prince’s concern about condemnation

Prince’s warning is not without biblical support:

- **Romans 8:1** — there is no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus.
- **Romans 5:1** — those justified by faith have peace with God.
- **Hebrews 8:12** — God promises not to remember the sins of His people under the new covenant.
- **1 John 2:1** — when believers sin, Christ is their advocate with the Father.
- **John 14:16, 26** — the Spirit is the Helper and Comforter.

These passages reject despair, rejection, and the belief that a believer loses justification every time he sins. They do not eliminate correction, confession, repentance, or discipline. In fact, Hebrews 12 and 1 John 1 show that assurance and correction belong together.

### Biblical distinction

| Biblical correction | Condemnation |
|---|---|
| “This action is sinful; turn back to God.” | “You are beyond forgiveness.” |
| Specific and truthful | Vague, crushing, and identity-based |
| Produces confession and repentance | Produces hiding, despair, or self-justification |
| Comes with the promise of cleansing and restoration | Denies the possibility of restoration |
| Consistent with Romans 8:1 and Hebrews 12 | Contradicts Romans 8:1 |

### Strongest biblical objection

The strongest single passage against Prince’s absolute wording is **Hebrews 12:5–11**. The writer addresses Christians as “sons” and says:

- God **reproves** His children.
- God **disciplines** those whom He loves.
- The purpose is that they may share His holiness.
- The result is “the peaceful fruit of righteousness.”

This is not condemnation from the accuser. It is loving, corrective action directed toward believers. Although the passage does not explicitly use the phrase “the Holy Spirit convicts believers of sin,” it directly disproves the idea that God never points out, confronts, or corrects the sins of His children.

The strongest supporting passage is **2 Corinthians 7:9–10**, where Paul says the Corinthians were “grieved into repenting” and calls their grief “godly.” The grief was not treated as demonic condemnation; it produced repentance and spiritual restoration.

The biblical objection can therefore be stated this way:

> Prince correctly rejects condemnation, but he wrongly treats all conviction, grief, reproof, and correction concerning sin as though they were condemnation. Scripture distinguishes them: condemnation drives a person away from God, while the Spirit’s corrective work brings the believer to confession, repentance, holiness, and restoration.

This is the central contradiction between Prince’s absolute statement and the full counsel of Scripture. The New Testament does not teach that believers are condemned for their sins, but it does teach that believers are reproved, disciplined, grieved over sin, called to confess, and led toward holiness.

### Final assessment against the full counsel of Scripture

If Prince means that the Holy Spirit does not **condemn** believers as rejected, unforgiven, or outside Christ, his pastoral concern is biblical.

If he means that the Holy Spirit never **convicts, reproves, exposes, or corrects** believers regarding sin, the statement is contradicted or seriously qualified by the broader biblical witness—especially 2 Corinthians 7:9–10, Hebrews 12:5–11, Revelation 3:19, 1 John 1:8–10, 1 Corinthians 11:28–32, Ephesians 4:30, and Romans 8:13.

The most faithful formulation is:

> The Holy Spirit does not condemn believers, but He does convict and correct them in order to lead them to confession, repentance, holiness, and deeper dependence on Christ.

## Historical doctrinal parallels

### Closest parallel: antinomianism

The closest historical parallel is **antinomianism**—the tendency to deny that God’s law has an ongoing role in exposing sin, producing repentance, or guiding Christian obedience.

The classic historical controversy occurred during the Reformation, especially between Martin Luther and Johannes Agricola. Agricola argued that repentance was produced by the Gospel rather than by the Law and that there was no proper place for the Law and its preaching in the Christian church. Luther rejected this position and maintained that the Law exposes sin and brings contrition, while the Gospel comforts the repentant conscience.

[The Antinomistic Controversy — Book of Concord](https://thebookofconcord.org/introductory-materials/historical-introductions/the-antinomistic-controversy/)

The resemblance to Prince’s teaching is seen in several points:

| Prince’s emphasis | Antinomian resemblance |
|---|---|
| The Holy Spirit does not convict believers of their sins. | The Christian does not need the Law or another condemning voice to expose sin. |
| The Spirit convicts believers of righteousness instead. | Repentance and Christian assurance are located almost exclusively in Gospel comfort. |
| Believers should not be sin-conscious. | Continued attention to the Law, sin, and contrition is treated as spiritually harmful or legalistic. |
| Grace, rather than law, is emphasized as the engine of holy living. | The Law’s continuing function in sanctification and obedience is minimized. |

This is a **doctrinal resemblance**, not proof that Prince is identical to Agricola. Prince does affirm holiness, says that grace produces changed living, and does not openly call Christians to practice sin. Therefore, the most accurate description is **hyper-grace with antinomian tendencies**, rather than simply labeling the entire ministry “Agricolan antinomianism.”

### Modern category: hyper-grace

“Hyper-grace” is a modern theological label, not the name of an ancient or formally defined church heresy. It is commonly used for teaching that strongly emphasizes complete forgiveness and freedom from condemnation while minimizing ongoing confession, repentance, discipline, the moral law, and the Spirit’s corrective work.

Prince’s statement about the Holy Spirit fits this modern category because it makes the believer’s righteousness the exclusive object of the Spirit’s conviction and treats conviction of sin as accusation or condemnation. The central concern is not that grace is emphasized, but that grace is made to exclude biblical correction.

### Historical labels that do not fit well

- **Marcionism:** This is not a good identification. Marcion rejected the Old Testament and taught that the God of the Old Testament was different from the Father revealed by Jesus. Prince does not appear to teach two gods or reject the Old Testament as Scripture.
- **Gnosticism:** There is no necessary connection to Gnostic teaching about salvation through secret knowledge, an evil material creation, or Christ merely appearing to be human.
- **Pelagianism:** This is almost the opposite error. Pelagianism emphasizes human ability and moral effort, whereas Prince strongly emphasizes grace and Christ’s finished work.
- **Nicolaitanism:** The identity and doctrine of the biblical Nicolaitans are too uncertain to use this label responsibly.

### Historical conclusion

The most responsible historical classification is:

> Joseph Prince’s teaching is not best identified with Marcionism, Gnosticism, or Pelagianism. Its closest historical family resemblance is to antinomianism, expressed in a modern hyper-grace form.

This does not by itself settle whether every doctrine taught by Prince is outside historic Christian orthodoxy. It does show that the claim “the Holy Spirit never convicts believers of sin” repeats the central antinomian tendency to separate Gospel comfort from the Law’s continuing work of exposing sin, producing repentance, and guiding holiness.

## Justin Peters on this specific teaching

Justin Peters directly addresses Joseph Prince in his sermon **“The Devilish Puppet Master of the Word-Faith Movement.”** Peters identifies Prince as a prominent Word-Faith teacher and critiques Prince’s treatment of repentance and sin.

[The Devilish Puppet Master of the Word-Faith Movement — Justin Peters](https://www.gty.org/sermons/print/TM13-10/the-devilish-puppet-master-of-the-word-faith-movement-justin-peters)

### Peters’s main objection

Peters acknowledges that *metanoia* can mean “a change of mind,” but argues that biblical repentance is more than adopting a positive outlook or changing one’s attitude. According to Peters:

- Genuine repentance is granted by the Holy Spirit.
- The Spirit produces godly sorrow over sin.
- Genuine repentance results in changed desires, conduct, and spiritual fruit.
- Repentance is not a human work that earns salvation, but God’s gracious work in the sinner.

This directly challenges Prince’s framework if Prince’s teaching is taken to mean that the Holy Spirit does not expose sin or produce sorrow over sin in believers.

### Peters’s own doctrinal position

Justin Peters Ministries states that the Holy Spirit convicts of sin, righteousness, and judgment, grants repentance, and produces ongoing progressive sanctification. It also describes justification as being evidenced by repentance from sin and progressive holiness.

[Justin Peters Ministries — Statement of Faith](https://justinpeters.org/statement-of-faith/)

Peters’s gospel summary similarly says that the Holy Spirit convicts of sin, righteousness, and judgment and grants repentance from sin through the Word of God.

[Justin Peters Ministries — The Gospel](https://justinpeters.org/the-gospel-2/)

### Godly sorrow versus condemnation

In his personal testimony, Peters distinguishes between a guilty conscience that produces worldly sorrow and godly sorrow that leads to repentance. He says that when God grants genuine repentance, sin grieves the believer, desires change, and a pattern of holiness begins to appear.

[Justin Peters — Testimony](https://justinpeters.org/about-justin/testimony/)

This distinction is important because Peters does not treat every painful awareness of sin as condemnation. His position is closer to the biblical distinction developed in this note:

| Justin Peters’s emphasis | Joseph Prince’s emphasis |
|---|---|
| The Spirit convicts of sin, righteousness, and judgment. | The Spirit convicts believers of righteousness, not their sins. |
| The Spirit grants godly sorrow and repentance. | Conviction of sin is associated with the accuser or conscience. |
| Repentance includes changed desires and visible fruit. | Believers should focus on their righteous standing rather than sin-consciousness. |
| Assurance and repentance belong together. | Assurance is emphasized in opposition to condemnation. |

### Assessment of Peters’s view

Peters’s critique supports the strongest biblical objection to Prince’s absolute wording. He preserves both sides of the biblical message:

1. Believers are justified by grace alone and are not under condemnation.
2. The Holy Spirit continues to expose sin, grant repentance, produce godly sorrow, and sanctify believers.

Peters would therefore likely regard Prince’s statement as a serious hyper-grace error when it is understood to deny the Spirit’s corrective work in believers. Peters’s criticism is not that Christians should live under shame, but that freedom from condemnation must never be confused with freedom from repentance, holiness, or the Spirit’s conviction.

## Historical and doctrinal note

Prince’s wording appears consistently in the materials identified above, although his emphasis is often framed as a contrast between the Holy Spirit’s work and the accuser’s condemnation. The most charitable reading is that he is rejecting condemning, shame-producing guilt—not denying every form of divine correction.

Nevertheless, his repeated use of absolute language makes the distinction difficult to maintain biblically. The wider New Testament teaches both **no condemnation for those in Christ** and **reproof, discipline, repentance, and holiness for believers**.
