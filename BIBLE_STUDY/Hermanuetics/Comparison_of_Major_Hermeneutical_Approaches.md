# Comparison of Major Hermeneutical Approaches

## Main Question

Hermeneutics asks: how should Scripture be interpreted faithfully, in context, and in a way that honors both the human author's intent and God's larger redemptive purpose?

## Quick Summary

### Historical-Grammatical
- Focuses on grammar, syntax, historical background, and literary genre.
- Seeks the author's intended meaning in the original context.
- Usually treats the "plain sense" as primary unless the text clearly signals symbolism.

### Redemptive-Historical / Christocentric
- Reads each passage within the unfolding story of redemption.
- Emphasizes promise and fulfillment, covenant development, and culmination in Christ.
- Guards against reading each text as an isolated moral lesson.

### Canonical
- Interprets passages in light of the final form of the whole canon.
- Pays close attention to how later Scripture develops earlier Scripture.
- Often overlaps with biblical theology.

### Allegorical / Spiritual
- Looks for symbolic or spiritual meanings beyond the immediate historical sense.
- Was influential in parts of patristic and medieval interpretation.
- Can produce rich theological reflection, but can also detach interpretation from authorial intent.

### Devotional / Application-First
- Reads Scripture with an immediate concern for personal transformation and obedience.
- Helpful for meditation, prayer, and discipleship.
- Becomes weak when application is made before careful exegesis.

## Important Distinction

- Hermeneutical **methods** are not the same as theological **systems**.
- For example, **dispensationalism** and **covenant theology** are larger theological frameworks that include hermeneutical commitments, but they are not just single interpretive techniques.
- In practice, many careful Bible students combine **historical-grammatical**, **canonical**, and **redemptive-historical** reading rather than using only one method.

## A Balanced Bible Study Workflow

### Step 1: Start with the text itself
- Observe grammar, repeated words, literary structure, historical setting, and genre.
- Ask what the author is saying before asking what the text means for today.

### Step 2: Place the passage in redemptive history
- Ask where the passage stands in the storyline from creation to new creation.
- Consider how it relates to covenant, promise, kingdom, temple, sacrifice, priesthood, and Christ.

### Step 3: Read it in the light of the whole canon
- Compare Scripture with Scripture.
- Let clearer passages help interpret more difficult passages.

### Step 4: Move to doctrine and application
- Ask what the passage teaches about God, humanity, sin, salvation, and obedience.
- Then make application that flows from the meaning of the text, not apart from it.

## Common Errors to Avoid

- Ignoring literary genre
- Making a verse mean something its context does not support
- Jumping to application before understanding the passage
- Finding hidden meanings with no textual control
- Reading later theology back into every earlier passage without care
- Treating personal impressions as equal to interpretation

## Study Notes

- A strong method usually begins with **historical-grammatical observation**.
- A mature Christian reading of Scripture should also account for the Bible's **unity**, **redemptive progression**, and **fulfillment in Christ**.
- **Allegorical interpretation** has had an important place in church history, but it becomes unsafe when it overrides the actual wording and context of the passage.
- **Devotional reading** is necessary for spiritual growth, but it is strongest when built on sound interpretation.
- A practical rule is: **exegesis first, theology next, application last**.
