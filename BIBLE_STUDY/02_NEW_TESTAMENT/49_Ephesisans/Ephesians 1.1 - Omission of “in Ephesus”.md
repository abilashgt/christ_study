# Omission of “in Ephesus” in Ephesians 1:1

## The textual variant

Most English Bibles translate Ephesians 1:1:

> To the saints who are **in Ephesus** and are faithful in Christ Jesus.

The words “in Ephesus” translate the Greek phrase **ἐν Ἐφέσῳ** (*en Epheso*).

However, the phrase is absent from several early and important witnesses. Without it, the opening reads approximately:

> To the saints who are also faithful in Christ Jesus.

This reading is grammatically possible, although it is less specific and somewhat unusual.

## Early evidence for the omission

The phrase is absent from:

- **Papyrus 46 (P46)**, generally dated around A.D. 175–225;
- **Codex Vaticanus (B)**, fourth century, in the original scribe’s text;
- **Codex Sinaiticus (א)**, fourth century, in the original scribe’s text;
- several other Greek witnesses, including some later manuscripts;
- some early patristic evidence, including the text known to Origen.

Later correctors added “in Ephesus” to Vaticanus and Sinaiticus. Most later Greek manuscripts contain the phrase, which explains its preservation in the majority of English translations.

The [NET Bible textual note on Ephesians 1:1](https://classic.net.bible.org/verse.php?book=Eph&chapter=1&theme=false&verse=1) summarizes the manuscript evidence and the major explanations for the variant.

## Early witnesses that include “in Ephesus”

The omission should not be confused with a lack of early support for the traditional reading. The phrase is found in important witnesses from the fifth century onward:

| Witness | Approximate date | Status of the reading |
|---|---:|---|
| **Codex Alexandrinus (A/02)** | Fifth century | Includes “in Ephesus” in the manuscript’s text. |
| **Codex Sinaiticus (א)** | Fourth century | The original hand omits the phrase; a later corrector adds it. |
| **Codex Vaticanus (B/03)** | Fourth century | The original hand omits the phrase; a later corrector adds it. |
| **Codex Claromontanus (D/06)** | Sixth century | Includes the phrase. This is a different manuscript from Codex Bezae, even though both are commonly designated by the letter D in different contexts. |

Other Greek witnesses commonly listed as including the phrase are **F, G, Ψ, 0278, 33, 1881**, and the Byzantine manuscript tradition (**𝔐**). A fuller critical-apparatus listing is **א², A, B², D, F, G, Ψ, 0278, 33, 1881, 𝔐**. Here **א²** and **B²** mean later correctors of Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, not their original scribes. The witnesses commonly cited for omission include **P46, א*, B*, 6, 424c, and 1739**. The exact list varies slightly by edition of the Greek New Testament and by whether corrections, marginal readings, and versional evidence are counted separately.

The earliest substantial Greek witness that clearly includes the phrase in the body of Ephesians 1:1 is therefore generally identified as **Codex Alexandrinus**, from the fifth century. The fourth-century Sinaiticus and Vaticanus do not provide independent original-hand support for the phrase; their later corrections reflect the reading that became standard in subsequent transmission.

This distribution creates the central textual question: the omission has strong support from the three earliest substantial witnesses—P46, Sinaiticus, and Vaticanus—while “in Ephesus” has broad support in later Greek manuscripts, early versions, and the continuing church tradition. A manuscript’s age is important, but textual critics also consider the quality, independence, and geographical distribution of its evidence.

## Why might the phrase have been omitted?

### 1. A circular-letter theory

The omission may indicate that Ephesians was intended to circulate among several churches in Asia Minor. The letter could have been copied with no specific city in the opening line, allowing each congregation to receive it as its own message.

This theory fits several features of the letter:

- Paul says that he has “heard of” the readers’ faith (Ephesians 1:15), even though he spent years ministering in Ephesus.
- In Ephesians 3:2 he writes, “if indeed you have heard” of his stewardship, which sounds less personal than his letters to churches he knew well.
- The letter contains no personal greetings, despite Paul’s long ministry in Ephesus.
- Ephesians is closely connected with Colossians, which was carried by Tychicus and may have circulated through churches in the region.

Ephesus was the most prominent city in the area. If it was the principal church through which copies circulated, its name could eventually have become attached to the letter in the manuscript tradition.

### 2. A scribal addition

Another explanation is that the original text had no city name and that a copyist later supplied “in Ephesus” because the letter became associated with that church. Copyists often clarified an apparently incomplete or unusual reading.

The traditional title “To the Ephesians” may have reinforced this development. A title, however, is not necessarily identical to the wording of the original letter’s opening line.

### 3. An intentional generalization

Some scholars have suggested that the phrase was deliberately removed from an early copy so that the letter could be read as a general Christian document. This is possible, but the agreement of several early witnesses makes the history difficult to reconstruct with certainty.

## Does the omission prove that Ephesians was not sent to Ephesus?

No. The omission shows that some of the earliest copies did not contain the phrase, but it does not establish the entire history of the letter’s destination.

Possible conclusions include:

1. The letter was a circular message for several churches.
2. Ephesus was the principal recipient, but other churches also received copies.
3. “In Ephesus” was added later because Ephesus became the best-known destination.
4. The phrase was original but was omitted in an early branch of the textual tradition.

The external evidence favors the omission, but the precise explanation remains uncertain. The evidence also does not prove that Ephesians was the letter to Laodicea mentioned in Colossians 4:16.

## Translation implications

Most modern translations retain “in Ephesus” in the main text and mention the omission in a footnote. Some editions use brackets or otherwise signal uncertainty. The textual issue does not materially change the central teaching of Ephesians concerning salvation in Christ, the unity of the church, or Christian conduct.

## Summary

The words “in Ephesus” are absent from Papyrus 46 and from the original hands of Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus. Their omission supports the possibility that the letter was written for circulation among multiple churches, but it does not disprove a historical connection with Ephesus. The traditional title is ancient and widely preserved, while the precise wording of Ephesians 1:1 remains textually uncertain.
