# Church vs Israel

## Main Question

What is the relationship between **Israel** and the **church** in God's redemptive plan?

The Bible presents both continuity and distinction:

- Israel is the nation descended from Abraham through Isaac and Jacob, to whom God entrusted the covenants, promises, law, worship, and the line of the Messiah ([[Romans 9:3-5]]).
- The church is the people gathered by faith in Jesus Christ from every nation, formed into one body by the Holy Spirit ([[Ephesians 2:11-22]]; [[1 Corinthians 12:12-13]]).
- Jewish and Gentile believers share salvation in Christ and become one new people, while the Bible still speaks of Israel as a distinct historical and theological entity ([[Romans 11:25-29]]).

The central issue is not whether Israel and the church have anything in common. They do. The main issue is whether the church **replaces Israel**, is **Israel expanded around Christ**, or is a **distinct people alongside Israel within one unified plan of redemption**.

## Unified Comparison: Israel and the Church

The word **foundation** can refer to several things: the beginning of a people, the covenant that establishes its identity, and the person or event on which its hope rests. Israel and the church have different historical foundations, but both ultimately depend on God's grace and the saving work of Christ.

### Israel's Foundation in More Detail

Israel's foundation should not be reduced to the Sinai covenant alone.

1. **God's sovereign call of Abraham** — God chose Abraham and promised to bless him, make him into a great nation, and bless all families of the earth through him ([[Genesis 12:1-3]]).
2. **The covenant line** — the promise continued through Isaac and Jacob rather than through every descendant of Abraham equally ([[Genesis 17:19-21]]; [[Romans 9:6-13]]).
3. **National redemption from Egypt** — God brought Jacob's descendants out of slavery and identified them as His treasured possession ([[Exodus 6:6-8]]; [[Exodus 19:4-6]]).
4. **The Sinai covenant** — Israel became a covenant nation under God's law, with priesthood, sacrifices, worship, and civil regulations ([[Exodus 24:3-8]]).
5. **The Davidic promise** — Israel's national hope was directed toward a royal Son whose kingdom would be established by God ([[2 Samuel 7:12-16]]).

Thus, Israel's foundation is both **familial** and **national**: Abraham is the covenant ancestor, while the exodus and Sinai establish Israel as a nation. Israel's deepest hope is not ethnic descent or law-keeping, but God's promise fulfilled through the Messiah from David's line ([[Matthew 1:1]]; [[Romans 9:5]]).

### The Church's Foundation in More Detail

The church is not founded on ethnic descent, a geographic territory, or the Mosaic law.

1. **Christ's person and work** — Jesus' incarnation, obedient life, atoning death, resurrection, and exaltation are the basis of the church's salvation ([[1 Corinthians 15:3-4]]; [[Ephesians 1:19-23]]).
2. **The apostolic and prophetic foundation** — the church is built on the authoritative testimony of the apostles and prophets ([[Ephesians 2:20]]; [[Acts 2:42]]).
3. **Christ as the cornerstone** — Jesus Christ aligns, supports, and gives meaning to the entire foundation; apart from Him, no other foundation can be laid ([[Isaiah 28:16]]; [[Ephesians 2:20]]; [[1 Corinthians 3:11]]).
4. **The new covenant** — Christ's blood secures forgiveness and creates the covenant people promised by the prophets ([[Luke 22:20]]; [[Hebrews 8:6-13]]; [[Hebrews 10:14-18]]).
5. **The Holy Spirit at Pentecost** — the Spirit unites believers into one body and empowers the church's worldwide witness ([[Acts 2:1-4]]; [[1 Corinthians 12:12-13]]).

### Was the Church Founded at Pentecost?

The New Testament shows both **preparation before Pentecost** and **public inauguration at Pentecost**.

- Jesus announced that He would build His church ([[Matthew 16:18]]).
- Jesus gathered and taught disciples during His earthly ministry ([[Mark 3:13-19]]; [[Luke 6:13-16]]).
- He established the Lord's Supper in the context of the new covenant ([[Luke 22:14-20]]).
- After His resurrection, He commissioned His disciples and promised the Holy Spirit ([[John 20:21-22]]; [[Acts 1:4-8]]).
- At Pentecost, the Spirit came upon the gathered disciples, and about three thousand people were added to the community ([[Acts 2:1-4, 41-47]]).

Therefore, Pentecost is best described as the church's **public Spirit-empowered inauguration**, while Jesus' ministry, death, resurrection, and promises are the necessary foundation and preparation. Different theological systems place the precise beginning of the church differently, but all orthodox views should affirm that Christ is its true foundation.

### Starting Point: Israel and the Church

Israel and the church have different starting points when “beginning” means the public formation of each people:

- **Israel:** Abraham is the covenant ancestor and beginning of the promise, but Israel becomes a formally constituted national covenant people when God redeems the descendants of Jacob from Egypt and establishes them at Sinai ([[Exodus 6:6-8]]; [[Exodus 19:3-6]]; [[Exodus 24:3-8]]).
- **The church:** Jesus announced and prepared the church during His earthly ministry, secured the new covenant through His death, rose from the dead, commissioned His disciples, and then publicly inaugurated the church through the Holy Spirit at Pentecost ([[Matthew 16:18]]; [[Luke 22:20]]; [[Acts 1:4-8]]; [[Acts 2:1-4, 41-47]]).

Some theological traditions use “church” broadly for God's people in both Testaments, while others use it more narrowly for the New Testament body of Christ. In the narrower and commonly used New Testament sense, **Pentecost is the church's public beginning**, just as the exodus and Sinai are Israel's national beginning.

### One Foundation, Different Historical Foundations

Israel and the church are not founded in exactly the same way:

- Israel is founded historically as Abraham's covenant family and then constituted as a nation through the exodus and Sinai.
- The church is founded historically on Christ's completed work, constituted as a new-covenant body through the Holy Spirit, and sent to all nations.
- Israel's institutions and promises prepare for and point toward Christ.
- The church does not create a second way of salvation; it announces the fulfillment of God's saving promise in Christ ([[Galatians 3:8, 14, 29]]).
- Both Israel and the church stand or fall by God's faithfulness, but the church's identity is explicitly centered on the crucified and risen Christ ([[1 Corinthians 2:2]]; [[1 Peter 2:5-6]]).

## What Israel Means in Scripture

The name **Israel** can refer to more than one related reality, so the context must be observed.

1. **Jacob personally** — God changed Jacob's name to Israel ([[Genesis 32:28]]).
2. **Jacob's physical descendants** — the twelve tribes and the Jewish nation ([[Exodus 4:22]]).
3. **The covenant nation** — the people brought out of Egypt and placed under the Mosaic covenant ([[Deuteronomy 7:6-8]]).
4. **The faithful remnant** — Israelites who truly trust God rather than merely possessing outward covenant privileges ([[Isaiah 10:20-22]]; [[Romans 9:6-8]]).

Israel's election was gracious and served a missionary purpose. Through Israel, God gave the Scriptures, the covenants, the promises, and the Messiah according to the flesh ([[Romans 3:1-2]]; [[Romans 9:4-5]]). Election did not mean that every individual Israelite was spiritually regenerate, nor did it make salvation possible apart from faith.

## What the Church Means in Scripture

The church is the assembly of those who belong to Jesus Christ. The New Testament uses the word **church** for both:

- local congregations ([[Acts 14:23]]; [[1 Corinthians 1:2]]), and
- the universal body of Christ, consisting of believers united to Him by the Spirit ([[Matthew 16:18]]; [[Ephesians 1:22-23]]).

The church includes believing Jews and Gentiles. Gentile believers are not saved by becoming ethnically Jewish; they are saved by grace through faith in Christ and are brought near by His blood ([[Ephesians 2:13]]). Jewish believers do not cease to be Jewish, but they and Gentile believers have equal standing in Christ ([[Galatians 3:28]]; [[Colossians 3:11]]).

## Important Similarities

Israel and the church are not two unrelated religions or two ways of salvation.

- Both exist by God's gracious calling.
- Both are called to worship the one true God.
- Both are recipients of God's revelation and promises.
- Both are commanded to live in holiness and faithfulness.
- The faithful in both eras are saved by grace through faith, on the basis of Christ's work ([[Genesis 15:6]]; [[Romans 4:1-8]]; [[Hebrews 11]]).
- The church receives the spiritual blessings of the promises fulfilled in Christ, including adoption, reconciliation, and access to God ([[Galatians 3:7-9, 26-29]]; [[Ephesians 2:19]]).

## Important Differences

### 1. National identity vs transnational identity

Israel is identified as a nation with a genealogy, tribal structure, land, civil order, and covenant history. The church is spread across nations and is not defined by one ethnicity, territory, or civil government ([[Acts 1:8]]; [[Philippians 3:20]]).

This does not mean Israel was saved merely by ethnicity, or that the church has no Jewish identity. It means that national and ethnic boundaries function differently in the two biblical categories.

### 2. The Mosaic covenant vs the new covenant

Israel stood under the Mosaic covenant, which regulated national life, priesthood, sacrifices, clean and unclean distinctions, festivals, and life in the land ([[Exodus 19-24]]; [[Leviticus 16]]).

The church lives under the new covenant established by Christ. Its members receive forgiveness through Christ's sacrifice and the indwelling Holy Spirit ([[Luke 22:20]]; [[Hebrews 10:10-18]]; [[2 Corinthians 3:6]]).

The new covenant was promised to the house of Israel and the house of Judah ([[Jeremiah 31:31-34]]). Gentile believers participate in its blessings through union with Israel's Messiah, but this fact must be handled alongside Paul's continued distinction between Israel and Gentiles in [[Romans 11]].

### 3. Temple-centered worship vs Christ-centered worship

Israel's Old Testament worship was organized around the tabernacle and temple, with a Levitical priesthood and animal sacrifices. These institutions pointed forward to Christ ([[Hebrews 8:1-6]]; [[Hebrews 9:11-14]]).

The church approaches God through Christ, the great High Priest, and offers spiritual sacrifices through Him ([[Hebrews 4:14-16]]; [[1 Peter 2:5]]). The church itself is described as God's temple because the Holy Spirit dwells in believers ([[1 Corinthians 3:16-17]]; [[Ephesians 2:21-22]]).

### 4. Circumcision and ethnic boundary markers vs faith in Christ

Circumcision marked the Abrahamic covenant and became a major identity marker in Israel ([[Genesis 17:9-14]]). The Jerusalem Council rejected the requirement that Gentile believers be circumcised and keep the Mosaic law in order to be saved ([[Acts 15:1-29]]).

In Christ, physical circumcision is not the basis of covenant membership or justification. What matters is faith working through love and the new creation ([[Galatians 5:6]]; [[Galatians 6:15]]; [[Philippians 3:3]]).

### 5. A national commission vs the Great Commission

Israel was called to display God's holiness and to be a light and blessing to the nations ([[Exodus 19:5-6]]; [[Isaiah 49:6]]). The church is explicitly commissioned to make disciples of all nations and to bear witness to Christ to the ends of the earth ([[Matthew 28:18-20]]; [[Acts 1:8]]).

The church's worldwide mission does not cancel Israel's place in God's promises. It shows that the blessing promised through Abraham is reaching the nations through Christ ([[Genesis 12:3]]; [[Galatians 3:14]]).

## Ephesians 2: One New Humanity

[[Ephesians 2:11-22]] is one of the clearest passages on the relationship between Jewish and Gentile believers.

- Gentiles were formerly alienated from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers to the covenants of promise.
- Through Christ's blood, those who were far off have been brought near.
- Christ has broken down the dividing wall of hostility.
- He has created **one new humanity** in place of the two.
- Both groups have access to the Father through one Spirit.
- Gentile believers are now fellow citizens with the saints and members of God's household.

The passage teaches genuine unity, not a separate Gentile salvation. It also assumes that there were formerly two identifiable groups. The church's unity is therefore a reconciliation in Christ, not proof that ethnic distinctions never existed or that every reference to Israel must now mean the church.

## Romans 9-11: The Key Passage for the Debate

Romans 9-11 must be read as a unit.

### Romans 9

Paul distinguishes between physical descent and saving faith: “not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel” ([[Romans 9:6]]). This guards against the idea that physical ancestry automatically guarantees salvation.

### Romans 10

Righteousness comes through faith in Christ. The same Lord is Lord of both Jews and Gentiles, and everyone who calls on Him will be saved ([[Romans 10:9-13]]).

### Romans 11

Paul describes Gentile believers as wild olive branches grafted into the rich root of the olive tree. They must not boast over the natural branches, because salvation is by faith and God is able to graft the natural branches in again ([[Romans 11:17-24]]).

Paul then speaks of a present partial hardening of Israel until the fullness of the Gentiles comes in and says that God's gifts and calling are irrevocable ([[Romans 11:25-29]]). Whatever one's eschatological position, this passage requires humility, rejects Gentile boasting, and takes God's faithfulness to Israel seriously.

### Israel's Present State: Partial Hardening

According to [[Romans 11:25-29]], Israel's present spiritual condition can be summarized as **partial hardening**.

- **Partial in extent** — the hardening is not experienced by every Israelite. Paul himself is an Israelite who believes in Christ, and throughout the New Testament there is a believing Jewish remnant ([[Romans 11:1-5]]; [[Acts 21:20]]).
- **Partial in scope** — Israel has not been cut off from every blessing of God's covenant history. Israel still possesses the historic privileges of adoption, glory, covenants, law, worship, promises, patriarchs, and the Messiah according to the flesh ([[Romans 9:4-5]]).
- **Partial in duration** — the hardening lasts “until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in” ([[Romans 11:25]]). Paul presents it as part of the present redemptive-historical period, not necessarily as Israel's final condition.
- **Partial in relation to faith** — the issue is rejection of the Messiah by many, not the impossibility of salvation for Jewish people. The gospel remains God's power for salvation to the Jew first and also to the Greek ([[Romans 1:16]]).
- **Partial in relation to God's purpose** — God continues to preserve a remnant and has not revoked His gifts or calling ([[Romans 11:5, 28-29]]).

The image of the olive tree teaches that Gentile believers have been grafted in among the natural branches. They stand by faith and must not become proud or despise the branches that were broken off. God is able to graft those natural branches in again ([[Romans 11:17-24]]).

This “partial hardening” describes Israel's spiritual condition in Paul's redemptive-historical argument; it should not be confused with a claim about every Jewish individual, every Jewish community, or the modern political State of Israel. The church's proper response is gospel witness, humility, prayer, and confidence in God's faithfulness—not contempt or boasting ([[Romans 11:20-21, 30-32]]).

### Ending Point: Israel and the Church

“Ending point” means the **eschatological fulfillment** of each people's calling, not the disappearance of one people from God's purposes.

- **Israel:** Israel's story reaches its goal in the Messiah promised through Israel. Scripture speaks of Israel's future salvation, restoration, resurrection, and participation in the consummation of God's kingdom ([[Romans 11:25-29]]; [[Acts 3:19-21]]; [[Ezekiel 37:21-28]]). Christians differ on whether land and national promises are fulfilled through a distinct future for national Israel, through Christ and the church, or through the final new creation.
- **The church:** In a pretribulational dispensational framework, the church age and its earthly mission end at the rapture. Christ gathers His church to Himself, and the church is then with Him while the prophetic program concerning Israel continues ([[1 Thessalonians 4:16-17]]; [[1 Corinthians 15:51-57]]). The church's final destination remains the resurrection life and eternal dwelling with God in the new creation ([[Revelation 21:1-4, 9-10]]).
- **Interpretive qualification:** Historic premillennial, postmillennial, and amillennial readings generally do not separate the rapture from Christ's visible second coming in the same way. They understand the church as remaining on earth until Christ's return and final resurrection. The rapture's timing is therefore a major point of disagreement within Christian eschatology.
- **Shared fulfillment:** Whatever distinctions remain in a particular theological system, the final hope of all who belong to Christ is centered on Christ's reign, resurrection life, God's presence, and the defeat of sin and death ([[Revelation 21:22-27]]; [[Revelation 22:1-5]]).

## Three Major Interpretive Positions

### 1. Replacement theology / supersessionism

In its strongest form, this view says that the church has replaced Israel and that Israel no longer has a distinct role in God's plan.

Some versions emphasize that the church inherits Israel's promises because Christ fulfills them. The danger of a strong form is that it can minimize [[Romans 11:17-29]], encourage Gentile boasting, or treat Jewish people as if they have no continuing significance in biblical history.

### 2. Covenant theology / fulfillment theology

This view emphasizes one unified people of God across redemptive history. The church is not a plan B; it is the community gathered around the promised Messiah. Many covenant theologians say that Old Testament promises reach their fulfillment in Christ and His people, while still allowing for a future conversion of many Jewish people.

The strength of this view is its emphasis on the unity of salvation, the centrality of Christ, and the way the New Testament applies Old Testament promises to the church. The key interpretive question is how specifically passages about Israel, land, kingdom, and restoration should be fulfilled in Christ and the new creation.

### 3. Dispensational distinction

This view maintains a stronger distinction between Israel and the church. Israel has a distinct national calling and future in God's plan, while the church is a distinct body formed at Pentecost and composed of Jewish and Gentile believers.

The strength of this view is its emphasis on God's faithfulness to His explicit promises to Israel and on the distinction maintained in [[Romans 11]]. The key interpretive question is how the unity of Jewish and Gentile believers in Christ ([[Ephesians 2:11-22]]) relates to a continuing distinction in God's future purposes.

## What the Church Does Not Mean

- The church is not a replacement for the Jewish Messiah; Jesus is Israel's Messiah and the church belongs to Him.
- The church is not saved by keeping the Mosaic law ([[Galatians 2:16]]).
- Gentile believers should not boast over Jewish people or assume that ethnicity makes them spiritually superior ([[Romans 11:18-20]]).
- The church is not a political nation-state with authority to impose Old Testament civil laws on every nation.
- The church does not erase the fact that God used Israel to give the world the Scriptures and the Messiah ([[Romans 9:4-5]]).
- Israel's historic privileges do not provide salvation apart from faith in Christ ([[John 8:24]]; [[Romans 10:1-4]]).

## A Careful Conclusion

The clearest biblical synthesis is:

1. There is one way of salvation: grace through faith in Jesus Christ.
2. The church is one body made up of believing Jews and Gentiles.
3. Gentile believers are fully accepted in Christ and are fellow heirs of God's promise.
4. Israel and the church should not be collapsed into the same category in every passage.
5. The church must reject both Jewish boasting and Gentile boasting.
6. God's promises must be interpreted through Christ, while God's faithfulness to Israel must be taken seriously.
7. Final conclusions about Israel's future should be made with humility, especially from [[Romans 11:25-29]].

## Study Questions

- In [[Ephesians 2:11-22]], what barriers between Jews and Gentiles has Christ removed?
- In [[Romans 11:17-24]], what does the olive-tree illustration affirm and what does it warn against?
- How does [[Galatians 3:7-29]] describe the relationship between Abraham's promise, faith, and Christ?

- How should the church's confidence in Christ be joined to humility toward Jewish people and toward questions of prophecy?
